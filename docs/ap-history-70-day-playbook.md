# The Spiral Studio

**A 70-day, evidence-based, end-to-end course design for AP World History: Modern (and APUSH)**

Built for: David Jacobson, Shanghai American School — 75-minute A/B blocks, ~70 teaching days per course, ~20 students per class, grades 10–12, near-native English speakers with full access to AI tools, SASPD competency-based grading.

Compiled August 2026 from five parallel research threads (cognitive science of learning; AP exam intelligence; books and curricula; classroom materials and tools; radical pedagogical designs), then checked against the toolkit you already run — the AP Theme Charts app (formerly SPICE-T; now organized by the six official CED themes — ENV, CDI, GOV, ECN, SIO, TEC), your AP skill suite (lesson planner, assessment builder, AI-resistant redesigner, discussion questions, student feedback + grading stress-test, UDL/CRP, sub plans), SASPD bands, and the HIPP/ACE routines. Every major claim carries an evidence tier. Fads are flagged as fads.

## Part 0 — The gameplan (one page)

**The name:** *Spiral Studio*. **Spiral** because the course revisits everything, on a schedule the memory research specifies, instead of covering-and-moving-on. **Studio** because class time is supervised practice and production — documents, writing, argument, defense — while first-exposure content moves out of the room. It is not a fad; every component is either Tier-1 evidence (causal, in relevant settings) or something you already run that the evidence endorses.

**The five commitments:**

1. **Content acquisition leaves the classroom.** Video/reading + a mandatory entry check at home; the 75 minutes are too scarce for first-exposure lecture. (Flipped-model meta-analytic g ≈ 0.5–0.7; on 70 days it's also just arithmetic.)
2. **Every block opens with cumulative retrieval.** 10–12 minutes, half current material, half from ≥2 units back, immediate feedback, calibration marks. This is the single highest evidence-per-minute activity known (classroom g ≈ 0.50; validated in actual history classrooms at roughly a letter grade of effect).
3. **Writing is trained in components, daily and weekly.** Sentence-level moves every block; SAQ/thesis/paragraph reps weekly; full essays about once per unit. Scored at scale through norming + peer/self-scoring + your existing feedback pipeline — not through you grading everything.
4. **Everything spirals toward May.** IDs and dates run on a successive-relearning schedule (3 correct now, relearn at ~2 weeks, ~5 weeks, April). Cross-period theme-chart comparisons are the interleaving engine. Review season is the last lap of a spiral that started in week one — not a separate unit.
5. **Major work is defended out loud.** 5–8 minutes, reasoning-scored. You already designed this in the AI-resistant redesigner; the Spiral Studio makes it the default for every major product, which simultaneously solves assessment integrity and deepens learning.

**What's genuinely new for you** (the rest is systematizing what you have): the retrieval/spacing scheduler, the flipped pipeline with accountability, the peer/self-scoring layer that buys 20+ writing reps per student, exam-weighted pacing, and a handful of high-value additions to the theme-chart app (Appendix D).

**What this replaces:** unaccountable reading homework, one-and-done unit tests, end-loaded review, teacher-graded-everything, and rereading/highlighting as "studying."

If you read nothing else tonight: Parts 1, 4, and 5.

## Part 2 — The evidence base (what survives scrutiny)

*The load-bearing science, compressed. Tiers: T1 = causal evidence in relevant settings · T2 = causal elsewhere, plausible transfer · T3 = practitioner consensus / case evidence.*

### 2.1 The two high-utility techniques — and the defaults they replace

Dunlosky et al. (2013, *PSPI*) rated ten learning techniques for generalizability. Only two rated **high**: **practice testing** and **distributed practice**. Rated **low** as typically used: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonics, imagery for text, and rereading — which surveys show are precisely students' defaults. Highlighting has *reduced* inference performance in some studies; rereading gains are front-loaded and vanish at delays.

### 2.2 Retrieval practice (T1 — including in real history classrooms)

- Meta-analytic: g ≈ 0.50 vs. restudy across 222 classroom studies (Yang et al. 2021); **feedback nearly doubles it** (Rowland 2014: g = 0.73 with vs. 0.39 without). Transfer to application/inference is real but moderate (Pan & Rickard 2018: d = 0.40) — retrieval builds the knowledge essays run on, but writing needs its own reps.
- In actual school history classes (Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel & McDermott 2011; Agarwal, Bain & Chamberlain 2012): quizzed content 94% vs. 81% on unit exams, 79% vs. 67% at semester's end — **about a letter grade**, and quizzing beat re-exposure, not just no-exposure. Students reported *less* test anxiety (72% of 1,408).
- Design constraints: aim ~70–80% success or attach immediate feedback; free/short-answer beats recognition; low stakes.
- The crossover that fools students: massed restudy *wins the immediate test* and loses the delayed one (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Teach this explicitly or students defect to rereading (Bjork's fluency illusion).

### 2.3 Spacing, with the actual schedule for a May exam (T1)

Optimal review gap ≈ 10–20% of the retention interval (Cepeda et al. 2008, n > 1,350). For an exam 3–5 months out: **review cycles at 2–4 week gaps**, and when in doubt space *longer* — too-long gaps degrade slowly, too-short gaps sharply. Direct history evidence: 8th-grade US history facts, 16-week-delayed review beat 1-week review at a 9-month test (Carpenter, Pashler & Cepeda 2009). Your A/B schedule's 48-hour gap between meetings is built-in spacing — an asset.

### 2.4 Successive relearning — the schedulable protocol (T1)

Rawson & Dunlosky (2011; 2022; Janes et al. 2020 — ≈ letter-grade gains in a real course):

> **Each item: 3 correct retrievals in session one → relearn to 1 correct in ~3 later sessions spaced 1–4 weeks apart**, gaps stretching toward the exam.

The catch that matters: when students self-regulate flashcards (drop cards early, pick their own timing), the benefit largely disappears. **The imposed schedule and mastery criterion are the intervention** — the app is incidental.

### 2.5 Interleaving — powerful, narrow (T1 in math; principled extrapolation here)

d = 0.83 in a preregistered math RCT (Rohrer et al. 2020), but the meta-analytic moderator is decisive: interleaving works for **discriminating confusable categories**, is null for expository text, negative for vocabulary (Brunmair & Richter 2019). History translation: mix the things students confuse — Ottoman/Safavid/Mughal, land vs. maritime empires, CCOT vs. comparison prompts, mixed-period SAQ sets. Don't interleave brand-new material; block first. **The theme-chart CompareView is this principle as software.**

### 2.6 The supporting cast

- **Self-explanation** (T1-ish: meta g ≈ 0.55, Bisra et al. 2018) — structured "explain why," not "explain everything." **Elaborative interrogation** works once a knowledge base exists.
- **Worked examples / cognitive load** (T2 for writing): annotated exemplar essays with scaffolds that *fade* as skill grows (expertise reversal). Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012, free) is the lesson-skeleton translation: open with review, small steps, many checks, ~80% success, weekly/monthly review.
- **Prequestions** before video/reading improve retention of what follows, even unasked material (Carpenter & Toftness 2017).
- **Metacognition**: exam wrappers alone are null (Soicher & Gurung 2017); what works is **feedback-driven metacognition** — after each quiz, sort items into knew / lucky / wrong and target accordingly. Practice tests replace "do I feel ready?" with evidence; overconfidence measurably produces underachievement.
- **Dual coding**: defensible only as Mayer's multimedia principles (coherent, aligned words + relevant visuals; no decorative clutter; don't read slides aloud). The EEF's classroom review found popular "dual coding" frequently null or harmful. Learning styles is a myth, full stop (Pashler et al. 2008).

### 2.7 Evidence hygiene

Treat Hattie's aggregate d-values and the 0.40 "hinge" as unreliable (Bergeron & Rivard 2017; Simpson 2017); the domain meta-analyses above are the load-bearing numbers. The EEF's 2021 applied review is the honest check: the principles are sound, classroom translations are under-evidenced, **implementation quality dominates**. And compression itself is survivable: intensive-format research finds parity with traditional formats *when* courses use active learning, tight structure, and continuous assessment; the one documented AP-specific risk is an unsupported gap before the exam (fall-semester AP), which your spring schedule avoids.

## Part 3 — Exam intelligence

*Sources tagged: [CB] = official College Board; [EST] = third-party estimate; [FOLK] = teacher-community consensus.*

### 3.1 Your students sit the NEW format — May 2027 [CB]

The AP history exam updates take effect **this cohort's exam**. What changes (apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-history-exam-updates):

- **SAQ:** all three required (choice gone), every one source-based — SAQ1 secondary text, SAQ2 primary text, **SAQ3 a non-text source (image/map/chart)**, each targeting a different period. Still 40 min / 20%.
- **LEQ:** one required prompt (the choice of three is gone), plus a brief orienting introduction suggesting areas for analysis. Same 6-point rubric, 40 min / 15%.
- **DBQ:** evidence may now come from **across the entire course** (effectively 1200–2001). Same 7-point rubric, 25%.
- **MCQ unchanged** (55 questions / 55 min / 40%). Rubrics and course content unchanged. Fully digital in Bluebook (since May 2025): typed essays, in-app highlighting, auto-submit.

**Strategic consequences:** (a) no more "pick your strongest LEQ period" triage — spiral review across *all* periods is now mandatory, which raises the value of keeping Units 1–2 alive all year and is exactly what the Spiral Studio does anyway; (b) **non-text source analysis becomes a guaranteed, drillable target** — add image/map/chart HIPP reps to the rotation; (c) train against the LEQ's orienting statement as a free scaffold; (d) current prep books all model the old format — the AP Classroom updated practice exams are the only fully current FRQ practice, **and your own assessments are ahead of the market here**: the field audit found your Unit 3 test already built to the 2027 format with non-text stimuli throughout. **No class sets of prep books this cycle.**

### 3.2 Unit weighting — where the exam spends its points [CB]

| Units | Weight |
|---|---|
| U1–U2 (1200–1450) | 8–10% each → 16–20% combined |
| **U3–U6 (1450–1900)** | **12–15% each → 48–60% — the heavy band** |
| U7–U9 (1900–2001) | 8–10% each → 24–30% |

Nothing after 2001 is tested (2025 Chief Reader says so explicitly). The calendar (Part 6) allocates blocks proportionally.

### 3.3 The score math — what a 5 actually requires [EST]

Composite = MCQ%×40 + SAQ%×20 + DBQ%×25 + LEQ%×15. Post-2024 cutoff estimates (College Board releases no worksheet; these are calculator projections): **5 ≈ 73%+, 4 ≈ 58%, 3 ≈ 42%**.

Worked example of a comfortable 5: **44/55 MCQ + 7/9 SAQ + 5/7 DBQ + 4/6 LEQ ≈ 75%.** A student can miss 11 MCQs, drop the DBQ's outside-evidence *and* complexity points, drop two LEQ points and two SAQ points — and still earn the 5. Perfection is not the goal; **banked cheap points at volume is the goal.**

Also decisive context: the 2024 "Great Recalibration" re-cut thresholds — **~48–50% of AP World and APUSH takers now earn a 4 or 5** (2025 AP World: 13.9/33.4/17.0 for 5/4/3; APUSH: 14.2/36.2/23.3). A well-taught class targets 4s and 5s aggressively; a 3 is no longer the benchmark of success.

### 3.4 Where the missing points actually live — 2025 Chief Reader data [CB]

2025 national DBQ point-earn rates (mean 3.61/7): **Thesis 0.80 · Doc evidence 1.61/2 · Contextualization 0.54 · Sourcing 0.29 · Evidence beyond the docs 0.19 · Complexity 0.17.**

Read that as a treasure map: thesis and doc-use are largely solved by ordinary instruction. **The differentiating points are outside evidence, sourcing, contextualization — in that order of scarcity — and contextualization is the cheapest to fix.** The complexity point (17%) is statistically near-irrelevant to reaching a 5; don't let students chase it at the expense of banked points.

**The Chief Reader's named failure patterns → the drills that answer them (all fold into Part 4.4's rotation):**

| CR failure pattern | The drill |
|---|---|
| **Quote-dumping** (worse since digital copy/paste) | The CR's own **redaction drill**: strip every quoted word from a draft — what argument remains? Run monthly |
| **Sourcing = restating the attribution line** | **Attribution-prediction**: from the source line alone, predict what the document will argue — then read and check. HIPP's H/I/P/P must connect *to the argument*, not just label |
| **Generic / out-of-period contextualization** | Context anchoring: name the watershed events bracketing the prompt period and explain how they created the prompt's conditions — as a standalone 3-sentence rep |
| **Outside evidence drought (0.19!)** | This is what the ID decks and relearning system are for: every ID card's "so what" field is a deployable piece of outside evidence. Drill: "prompt → 3 pieces of outside evidence in 90 seconds" |
| **Chronology silos** (facts locked in one unit) | Period-classification sprints (the CR recommends this verbatim); cross-period theme-chart comparisons |
| **Rubric-gaming backfires** | CR data: essays using **all 7 docs and sourcing 4+** outperform minimal-compliance essays on *every* point. Teach abundance, not compliance |
| **SAQ prompt-verb misreads** ("ideology" ≠ a person; "explain" ≠ "identify") | Prompt-verb drills; ACE already handles structure — aim it at the right noun |
| **Monocausal arguments** | Because/but/so (4.4) is multi-causality training at sentence level; "two-sided effects" as the default LEQ frame |
| **LEQ time expiry** | Timed reps are non-negotiable; the 40-minute clock is trained like a distance event |

## Part 4 — The Spiral Studio architecture

Seven components. Each tagged **HAVE** (you run it — keep/extend), **EXTEND** (you have the seed), or **NEW**.

### 4.1 The flipped content pipeline — NEW (T2)

All first exposure moves home: **Heimler video or WHP AP article (+ audio/leveled versions where useful) + a mandatory accountability artifact** — 3–5 question entry quiz or a completed retrieval guide checked at the door. The consistent moderator in the flipped meta-analyses (Li et al. 2025, g = 0.53; humanities subgroups higher): it works **iff** there's a pre-class check and the freed time goes to active practice with feedback. Unaccountable reading gets ~20–30% compliance; checks roughly double it.

Your WHP alignment file already maps lessons to CED topics — the pipeline is an assembly job, not a build. *Shanghai check:* Heimler free videos are YouTube-hosted; his paid packet streams from his own platform — verify from campus. WHP/OER, Freemanpedia (Squarespace), and AP Classroom are the reliable layer; AMSCO in print is the failsafe.

### 4.2 Daily cumulative retrieval — NEW (T1, the backbone)

Every block opens with 10–12 minutes of retrieval, **cumulative by design**:

- **Lagged opener** (4–6 items): ~half current unit, ~half from ≥2 units back. No/low stakes. Immediate whole-class feedback.
- Rotating formats: brain dump ("everything you know about the Columbian Exchange," 3 min, pair-compare — free recall also improves *organization* of knowledge, Zaromb & Roediger 2010); map-from-memory; timeline placement; empire-discrimination sets (the interleaving that fits history); Two Things.
- **Calibration layer:** students mark each item knew / lucky / wrong and keep a running tracker — feedback-driven metacognition, and the raw material for their April review plan.

### 4.3 The successive-relearning system — NEW (T1)

- **~40–60 IDs per unit max** (CED illustrative examples, not everything) + one **~50-date "spine of the course" deck** + process cards (HIPP moves, rubric points, ACE).
- Card grammar: term → what / **so what** / period + region.
- Students build the cards (generation effect); you audit.
- **Imposed schedule** (because self-regulated flashcarding loses the effect): to 3-correct in the unit's first week → relearn to 1-correct at ~2 weeks → ~5 weeks → April spiral. Tools ranked by mechanism: Anki > Knowt > Quizlet; Gimkit best of the games (resurfaces missed items); Kahoot fine in team mode; Blooket is engagement theater. Paper works. The schedule is the product — which is why this belongs in the app (Appendix D).

### 4.4 Component writing, daily and weekly — EXTEND (T3 + deliberate-practice logic; HIPP/ACE are the grammar)

- **Daily (5 min):** sentence-level moves à la Hochman/*The Writing Revolution* — **because/but/so** expansions on historical claims ("The Ottomans expanded rapidly *because*… / *but*… / *so*…" is causation practice, retrieval, and complexity-sentence construction at once); sentence combining with unit vocabulary; subordinate-clause drills using rubric frames ("Although X, Y because Z").
- **Weekly:** SAQ reps (ACE) 1–2×; thesis-only reps ("10 theses in 20 minutes"); single body paragraphs; 2-document HIPP drills. Components before wholes — reps on parts beat reps on full essays for skill acquisition.
- **Per unit:** one full timed DBQ (once DBQ instruction starts) or LEQ; **worked example first** — every new skill opens with an annotated College Board sample and a scaffold that fades.
- Your near-native students don't need language scaffolds — they need **rubric-move precision**. Same drills, aimed at the complexity point instead of at English.

### 4.5 Spiral assessment + the theme-chart app as interleaving engine — HAVE/EXTEND (T1 mechanism)

- Every quiz and prompt cumulative by design; themes revisited as comparison lenses across periods.
- **Theme-chart cadence:** each new empire/period gets a chart (generative study); each unit closes with a CompareView against a *prior-period* chart plus CCOT annotations — that cross-period comparison is simultaneously interleaving, spacing, and LEQ prep. The tutor's constraint (reasons only over the student's own entries) makes chart quality visible: thin chart, thin tutoring session — itself feedback.
- Unit tests: ~30% prior-unit material, announced as policy from day one. One-and-done is over. (Working precedent from your own materials: the Unit 3 test already embeds 5/25 MCQs from Units 1–2 by pacing-guide design — the 30% target is a turn of an existing dial, not a new ask.)
- **Running Theme Tracker** (backported from your field materials — stronger than the ID-card "so what" field for theme continuity): one page per CED theme, each a 9-row table (one row per unit, CONTINUED vs CHANGED), filled in ~15 minutes at every unit close. By April each page shows one theme moving across the whole course — ready-made CCOT and LEQ evidence. Standard course artifact; also a natural future app feature (Appendix D).

### 4.6 Anchor experiences — EXTEND (T1 by extrapolation + T3)

Three to four per year, project-shaped but exam-aimed, in the Knowledge in Action mold (the one radical architecture with RCT evidence of raising AP qualifying rates — +8–10pp in AP Gov/APES; no history RCT yet, flagged honestly):

- **Empires Summit** (Units 3–4): teams own an empire, build the theme chart, defend comparative claims before a panel; the artifact feeds directly into the Unit 3/4 LEQ.
- **Decision-point simulations** (short, one block): "You are the Qianlong Emperor; the Macartney mission waits outside." Evidence favors short decision-point formats over multi-week immersions for retention per hour. One Reacting-style microgame max per semester (the 1911 Revolution game is locally resonant).
- **Museum exhibit with curatorial argument** (Units 7–9): sources + captions + a defended thesis about selection.
- **Structured Academic Controversy** where a genuine controversy exists: pairs master one side, present, **swap sides**, seek consensus — the side-swap is complexity-point training (Johnson & Johnson research base).

Each anchor runs through your AI-resistant redesigner pattern by default: checkpoints, classroom-anchored evidence, process trail, defense.

### 4.7 Oral defense as the default — HAVE, systematized (T3 + Denmark precedent)

Every major written product gets a 5–8 minute defense; the essay is evidence, the defense is the assessment. Reasoning-scored (SASPD Argumentation/Claims bands), questions previewed. With ~20 students and an A/B schedule, one class's defenses fit inside two blocks' studio segments, or run as conference-style rotations during anchor-project work time. This is your redesigner's Section C promoted from option to operating system — and it's the integrity architecture that makes take-home AI use a non-issue: AI can draft words; it can't defend them in the student's mouth.

## Part 5 — What class actually looks like

### 5.1 The five day types

**Type A — Studio Day (the default, ~50% of days)**

| Min | Segment | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 0–12 | Cumulative retrieval opener | Lagged quiz or brain dump → immediate feedback → knew/lucky/wrong marks |
| 12–17 | Sentence sprint | because/but/so or rubric-frame drill on current content |
| 17–30 | Targeted direct instruction | The 10-minute version: clarify what the entry quizzes showed was misunderstood — not first exposure. Mayer-clean slides |
| 30–60 | Studio block | theme-chart work, 2-document HIPP drill, peer-instruction stimulus MCQs (commit → pair argument → revote), or anchor-project work — rotating |
| 60–72 | Component writing rep | SAQ or thesis rep; some days peer-scored on the spot |
| 72–75 | Exit + preview | One retrieval item + one calibration judgment; prequestions for tonight's video |

**Type B — Document Day (~1 per week)**
Opener (12) → RLH-style document lesson around a central historical question — the Reading Like a Historian model, the strongest-evidenced history-specific curriculum in existence (Reisman 2012: gains in historical thinking, factual knowledge, *and* reading transfer) — using DIG lessons or your discussion-questions skill for the tiered question set (25–40) → whole-class argument over the central question (15) → written synthesis: one ACE paragraph answering the question (10).

*Field-proven B-day variant — the **recall-check anchor** (backported from your Unit 3 materials): a multi-day sequence — DBQ skills primer with a collected prediction write → document working session with half the room on closed laptops and half directing an AI → a surprise, silent, handwritten free-recall check the next class ("write everything you remember"). A designed retention experiment and an integrity architecture in one; run it once per semester and keep the recall data.*

**Type C — Writing Workshop Day (~3 per unit once essay instruction starts)**
Opener (10) → worked example: annotate a real scored CB sample against the rubric, class scores it first (15) → timed component or full write (25–40) → norm-and-score: self-score with rubric, swap for peer score, discrepancies argued to the rubric text (15). You collect everything, spot-score a third, whole-class feedback next block.

**Type D — Summit/Defense Day (anchor milestones, ~6–8 per year)**
Defenses run conference-style: 3 stations, panel = you + 2 trained peers per station, 8-minute slots; non-presenting students have a structured evaluator role (scoring one rubric row + writing one question they'd ask). Twenty students clear in two blocks.

**Type E — Spiral Day (1 per ~2 weeks, and most sub days)**
Full-period mixed retrieval and repair: interleaved MCQ sets from AP Classroom question bank → error autopsy in pairs → relearning session on each student's knew/lucky/wrong backlog → mixed-period SAQ. Runs fine with a sub (self-contained packet — your sub-plans skill builds it).

### 5.2 A model week (A/B block, one course)

- **Block 1 (Mon):** Studio Day. Home before: Heimler 4.5 + entry quiz. In class: opener with Unit 2 lag items; sentence sprint on Atlantic economies; 12-min clarification of entry-quiz misconceptions; theme-chart block (Portuguese trading post empire); thesis reps ×3; exit + prequestions.
- **Block 2 (Wed):** Document Day. RLH-style set on the Middle Passage sources; tiered discussion; ACE paragraph out the door.
- **Block 3 (Fri):** Studio Day. Opener (lag: Unit 1 + this week); HIPP drill on 2 documents; peer-instruction stimulus MCQs; SAQ rep peer-scored; card session logged (3-correct on this week's IDs).
- **Weekend homework:** one WHP article + retrieval guide; 15-minute relearning session (app-scheduled, 2-week-old deck).

### 5.3 The unit template (≈ 6–8 blocks for a mid-weight unit)

1. **Launch block:** unit prequestions + framing narrative (your one real lecture — 25 minutes, Mayer-clean, the story arc of the unit) + first chart opened.
2. **3–5 Studio/Document blocks** on the topic sequence (flipped pipeline running underneath).
3. **Writing Workshop block(s):** unit-relevant skill focus (one rubric row at a time).
4. **Close block:** cross-period CompareView + CCOT annotations in the app; unit check (30% lagged content, mastery-retake eligible); defense slots if the unit carried an anchor.
5. **Week +2 and +5:** the unit's ID deck resurfaces automatically in openers and relearning sessions. Nothing is ever "done."

### 5.4 Where your skills fire in the rhythm

Sunday planning: `frame` → `ap-world-lesson-planner` (briefed with the Studio skeleton) for the week's blocks; `ap-world-assessment-builder` for the unit check and any rubric; `ap-world-discussion-questions` before Document Days; `ap-world-ai-resistant-redesigner` at each anchor launch; `grading-stress-test` → `ap-world-student-feedback` on gradebook-bound essays only; `ap-world-udl-crp` once per unit plan; `close` at week's end. ## Part 7 — Homework (what pays, what doesn't)

**The only four homework types in the course:**
1. Flipped video/article + entry accountability (the content pipeline).
2. Scheduled relearning sessions (15 min, the app tells them which deck).
3. One component write or a 90-second recorded argument defense (integrity-friendly, speaking practice).
4. Prequestion sheets before new topics.

**Abolished, with the evidence as cover:** unaccountable textbook reading, Cornell-notes-for-points, definition copying, "study for the test" (replaced by scheduled relearning — *studying is now a specified behavior, not a vibe*).

**Teach the science of itself:** 20 minutes early on Bjork's fluency illusion, revisited when retrieval feels harder than rereading. It feels harder because it's working. Students who believe this stop defecting.

## Part 9 — Grading and feedback at scale (~20 students, 3 courses, one you)

The principle: **teacher scoring is for calibration and the gradebook; volume comes from norming + peers + self + AI-formative.** The meta-analytic cover: peer assessment g ≈ 0.28–0.31 with peer–teacher correlations ~.63–.69 *rising sharply with rater training* (Double, McGrane & Hopfenbeck 2020); self-assessment interventions positive (Yan et al. 2022).

1. **Norming first (game film):** before students are ever peer-scored, they score anchor sets — a 7, a 4, a 2 from CB samples — and argue discrepancies to the rubric text. This is itself among the highest-value writing instruction you can run.
2. **Peer scoring** on weekly component reps, rubric-row-limited ("today we only score evidence").
3. **Self-scoring by sample-matching:** rank three anonymized responses, place your own, justify in rubric language.
4. **Whole-class feedback:** read all, mark nothing, one patterns-slide + targeted rewrite next block (the EEF marking review found no evidence detailed written marking beats lighter approaches).
5. **Your pipeline** (grading-stress-test → student-feedback) reserved for: gradebook-bound essays, defense scoring disputes, and monthly calibration spot-checks of the peer layer.
6. **AI-formative (Class Companion et al. or your own stack):** unlimited revision reps *before* submission; **never touches the gradebook** — 2024 accuracy research has AI matching human essay scores only ~30–40% of the time. Consistent with your book's whole thesis.
7. **SASPD mechanics:** mastery re-takes on objective checks; unit deadlines hard ("mastery within, deadlines between" — the bounded version of mastery learning that survives a fixed exam date; open self-pacing does not).

## Part 10 — The AI layer (your unfair advantage)

The credible AI-tutoring results (Kestin et al. 2025 Harvard physics RCT: ~2× gains in less time; World Bank Nigeria: ~0.3σ from supervised structured sessions) share three conditions: the AI serves a **structured curriculum** rather than inventing one; sessions are **scheduled and supervised**; the tutor is **pedagogically constrained** — Socratic, stepwise, never answer-dumping. That is a description of the app's existing guardrails. Local-first WebLLM additionally clears the network/privacy constraints that make cloud AI awkward in China.

The honest flags: the Kestin effect comes from convergent physics problems — transfer to contested historical interpretation is unproven. And the cautionary tale is Alpha School: "2-hour learning / 2.6× growth" is internally computed, unverified, incentive-confounded, and the platform is adaptive drill software — an existence proof of marketing, not learning. Your instinct (AI reasons over the student's own work, never generates content) is the defensible position, and the classroom architecture that makes it stick is Part 4.7's oral defense.

Where AI legitimately sits in the Spiral Studio: tutor-guided chart revision ("interrogate my Unit 4 chart"), retrieval-item generation over the OER knowledge base, formative essay feedback pre-submission, and the relearning scheduler. Appendix D turns this into a build list.

## Part 11 — How far to turn the dial

Three coherent versions; all share Parts 4–9. Pick per course, not globally — APWHM and APUSH can run different settings.

- **Setting 1 — Spiral Studio (recommended default):** everything in Parts 4–9 as written. Radical only in completeness; nearly all Tier-1 or already-yours. Near-zero downside risk.
- **Setting 2 — Studio + Summits:** Setting 1 with the full anchor-experience program (4 anchors, all defended, conference-style). The KIA extrapolation lives here; the cost is ~8 blocks of the calendar, paid for by the flipped pipeline. If May scores hold or rise in year one, keep; the KIA RCTs found effects *grew* in teachers' second year.
- **Setting 3 — The AI-Tutored Flip:** Setting 2 plus content acquisition restructured around the constrained tutor over the unit knowledge bases, with mastery checks gating and class time fully reallocated to studio/defense. Frontier territory — you'd be generating the evidence, which, given the book, might be exactly the point. Run it as a design experiment with the measurement plan in Part 12, one course only.

**Refused regardless of setting, with reasons on file:** open self-pacing against a fixed exam date (pace collapse; Modern Classrooms' achievement evidence is perception surveys); thematic resequencing of the units (the CED's chronology is baked into the exam — spiral the *assessment*, not the syllabus); any grading reform sold as raising scores; unaudited AI grading; multi-week immersion simulations; Thinking Classrooms as a framework (weak evidence — though vertical whiteboards + visibly random groups for document work are free to steal); learning-styles anything.

## Part 12 — Measurement (how you'll know)

1. **Leading indicators:** per-student cumulative-quiz retention curves; rubric-point acquisition (which points, which students); calibration accuracy (predicted vs. actual — should converge by November).
2. **AP Classroom Progress Checks** per unit as the standardized formative; question-bank lagged sets as the spiral signal.
3. **Two full mocks** in review season under Bluebook conditions, 2027-format papers.
4. **Steal the Agarwal design:** quiz half a unit's IDs and not the other half; compare on the unit check. Twenty students is enough to see the letter-grade gap — and it's a chapter for the book.
5. **Autopsy in July:** score report by unit and question type against the calendar's day allocations; feed next year.
